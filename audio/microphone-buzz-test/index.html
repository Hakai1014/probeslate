Quick guide

How to use the microphone hum test

1

Select your microphone

Choose the input that actually has the buzzing or hum problem. Wired headset, USB and built-in microphones can all be tested.

2

Stay quiet during measurement

The test listens to the background signal for several seconds and checks whether low-frequency tones persist over time.

3

Read the pattern, not just one peak

A single 50 Hz or 60 Hz peak is not enough. The tool looks for multiple persistent, harmonically related components.

Understanding the output

What does the result mean?

No clear pattern

The measurement did not find enough persistent harmonically related tones to identify a 50 Hz or 60 Hz mains-like family. This does not mean the microphone is completely noise-free.

Mains-like pattern detected

Several persistent components line up with a 50 Hz or 60 Hz harmonic family. This can be consistent with electrical interference, but acoustic motors, fans and other tonal sources can imitate the same spectrum.

Low or medium confidence

The evidence is incomplete, ambiguous or affected by browser/device limitations. Treat it as a clue rather than a hardware diagnosis.

Troubleshooting context

Why can a microphone buzz or hum?

Microphone buzzing can enter anywhere in the capture chain — the microphone capsule, an analog headset cable, a 3.5 mm jack, USB power, an audio interface, the computer's power system or the surrounding acoustic environment. A browser spectrum test cannot identify the physical cause from one measurement alone.

Laptop power or grounding pathA charger or power state can sometimes change the measured pattern, which is why the tool offers an optional same-session comparison.
Analog cables and connectorsPoor contact, shielding or an analog input stage can introduce tonal interference or broadband noise.
USB / interface powerUSB microphones and audio interfaces can pick up interference through their power or signal path.
Fans, HVAC and motorsA microphone hears acoustic pressure. Mechanical sources can produce 50/60 Hz-related tones and harmonics that resemble electrical hum.

Transparent methodology

What does this test actually measure?

The tool uses the browser's Web Audio API and analyzes the selected microphone locally. In the current measurement engine, a 32,768-sample FFT is evaluated in overlapping temporal frames. Rather than declaring hum from one spectral spike, each target frequency is evaluated over time.

  • 50 Hz family: 50, 100, 150, 200 and 250 Hz.
  • 60 Hz family: 60, 120, 180, 240 and 360 Hz.
  • Temporal persistence: median prominence and the lower quartile (P25) are used so brief spikes are less likely to count as persistent evidence.
  • Harmonic requirement: at least two persistent components are required before a mains-like family is classified.
  • Browser processing: echo cancellation, noise suppression and automatic gain control are requested off where supported. If processing remains active, confidence may be reduced.

This is an engineering diagnostic heuristic for consumer browsers, not a calibrated electrical instrument, acoustic standard measurement or proof of a specific hardware fault.

Why 50 Hz or 60 Hz?

Why does electrical hum often appear at these frequencies?

AC mains systems operate at a nominal line frequency that is commonly 50 Hz or 60 Hz depending on the country. Interference can also appear at harmonics such as 100/120 Hz and higher multiples. Power-supply behavior may make a harmonic more prominent than the fundamental, which is why the test examines a family of frequencies instead of relying on only 50 Hz or 60 Hz.

FAQ

Microphone hum test questions

Can this prove that I have a ground loop?

No. A mains-like spectrum is evidence about the audio signal, not proof of a particular physical cause. Comparison tests can provide clues, but they cannot prove that a charger, cable or ground loop is faulty.

Can I use a headset microphone?

Yes. The test analyzes whichever microphone input you select. With an analog headset, the measured signal includes the entire capture path: microphone, cable, connector, computer audio input and browser/OS processing.

Why does the test ask me to stay quiet?

The goal is to measure persistent background tones. Speech and other changing sounds add spectral energy that is unrelated to the hum being tested.

Does a result of “No clear pattern” mean my microphone is perfect?

No. It only means the test did not identify enough persistent 50/60 Hz-family evidence. The microphone may still have hiss, clipping, intermittent static or other noise that this specific tool is not designed to classify.

Why can a fan affect the result?

A microphone cannot know whether a tone came through an electrical path or arrived acoustically. Fans, HVAC systems and motors can create persistent low-frequency tones and harmonics.
